Voice AI design guide

Six principles of voice design

Designing for the ear is different from designing for the screen. These six principles come from conversation research and translate directly into how a voice agent should talk, listen and recover.

13 example calls, each with a transcript. Headphones help.

For years, voice bots were held back by the technology: shaky language understanding, no memory between turns, and conversations that fell apart after one or two exchanges. Large language models have fixed most of that. The bottleneck now is design.

Voice is less forgiving than chat. Callers notice every pause, every mispronounced name and every awkward hesitation. There are no buttons, links, forms or visual hierarchy to lean on, only words heard once in order. The principles below draw on conversation analysis, the study of how people actually organise talk, rather than on persona or tone alone.

Where design lives

Three layers of a voice agent

Each principle shows up in one or more of these layers. The "How to build it" notes under each principle are tagged with the layer they belong to.

Prompt

Prompt instructions

Concrete rules for how each turn is built: what goes first, where the question goes, how long a turn can be. Specific rules work far better than tone words like "friendly".

Logic

Conversation logic and state

Variables, slot-filling, retry counters and confirmations that run outside the model's reasoning, so they behave the same way every time.

Channel

Voice channel settings

Voice choice, speech speed, wait cues, barge-in, recognition hints and call routing. These shape how the agent sounds and how quickly it responds.

Before you start

Choosing a voice

Pick the voice against a written persona spec, not by scrolling a voice library until something sounds nice. Voices and speech models change quickly, so write down what you need (language, accent, pacing, warmth, how it handles your domain terms) and test candidates against that.

  • Test pronunciation of your real vocabulary: product names, place names, currency amounts, account and order formats.
  • Tune speaking speed and stability on real scripts. Small changes are very audible on the phone.
  • Set the wait-cue trigger low, around 500 ms, so callers are never left in silence.
  • Check which delivery controls your chosen model supports, since not every model exposes the same settings.
Principle 1

Manage how long the wait feels

On a call, silence is a signal. After about half a second to a second of dead air, callers start to wonder if the line dropped. A typical speech-to-text, model, text-to-speech pipeline can add a second or more before any backend lookup even starts.

You often can't remove the delay, but you can change how it feels. Say something before the wait starts, fill longer waits with a soft audio cue, and give a progress update if a lookup runs long. When the wait is predictable, tell the caller roughly how long it will take.

In practice

  • Acknowledge the request before calling any slow system: "Sure, let me pull that up."
  • Play a soft, non-verbal cue during waits longer than about a second.
  • For lookups over a few seconds, give a short progress update instead of more silence.
  • Write filler lines in the agent's own voice. A formal banking agent and a casual food-delivery agent shouldn't sound the same while they wait.

How to build it

  • PromptInstruct the agent to acknowledge every request that triggers a lookup before it calls the tool.
  • LogicMark slow actions so the platform plays a progress message or cue while they run. Check the default, since many platforms leave it off.
  • ChannelSet the wait-cue trigger to around 500 ms. Defaults of several seconds leave callers in silence far too long.
Principle 2

Design for turn-taking

People don't wait for silence to know it's their turn. They predict where a turn will end from grammar, intonation and context, and they come in at those points. Conversation analysts call them transition-relevance places.

A voice agent needs clear places where the caller can take over, and it needs to give way when they do. A caller who interrupts isn't breaking the system. Usually they've heard enough and know what they want.

In practice

  • Keep agent turns to one or two short sentences so there's a natural point to respond.
  • When the caller barges in, stop speaking immediately.
  • Treat what the caller said during the interruption as their new turn, and answer it.
  • Don't restart the interrupted list or prompt from the top.

How to build it

  • PromptTell the agent that an interruption replaces its current turn: stop, respond to what the caller said, and don't repeat what was cut off.
  • PromptLimit spoken turns to one or two short sentences.
  • ChannelKeep barge-in on. Leave end-of-speech detection at its default unless testing shows callers are being cut off.
Principle 3

Put the question last

In spoken English, new information usually comes at the end of a sentence, and listeners respond to the last thing they heard. If the question comes first and more detail follows, callers answer the detail.

Order each turn so context comes first, caveats come before the thing they qualify, and the question comes at the very end. When this clashes with a style rule, like "be concise" or "lead with the answer", information order wins. Ask for one thing per turn.

In practice

  • End the turn with the question. Nothing should follow it.
  • Give context and conditions before the offer, not after.
  • State caveats before the option they apply to.
  • Ask for one action per turn.

How to build it

  • PromptWrite ordering rules into the agent's instructions: context first, caveats before options, the question last, one question per turn.
  • LogicInsert situational details, like fees or eligibility, through conditions so they appear in the right place in the turn.
Principle 4

Go easy on working memory

A listener can't scroll back. Speech is heard once, so everything the agent says has to fit in the caller's short-term memory. Ask for three things at once and callers answer one, then ask what the others were.

Ask for one piece of information at a time. If the caller volunteers extra details, keep them and don't ask again. Read out no more than three options. Confirm details lightly by repeating them in the next question, and save full read-back confirmation for actions that are hard to undo.

In practice

  • Ask one question per turn.
  • Capture details the caller volunteers early so you don't ask for them again.
  • List no more than three options, and offer to read out more.
  • Confirm implicitly by repeating what you heard in the next question.
  • Confirm explicitly only before high-stakes actions: bookings, payments, cancellations.

How to build it

  • LogicTrack each required field as a variable, so the agent knows what it already has.
  • LogicLet the agent fill several fields from one caller sentence when the caller volunteers them.
  • PromptEnforce one question per turn and a final summary before committing.
Principle 5

Repair in steps

Mishearing is normal in conversation, and people fix it all the time. They start with the lightest fix that might work and only escalate if it doesn't.

A voice agent should do the same. Start with a light check ("Sorry?"), then name what was missed ("I didn't get the last name"), then offer a guess to confirm. For names, codes and numbers, switch to spelling or digit-by-digit input. Never repeat the same failed prompt word for word, and never suggest it's the caller's fault.

In practice

  • First attempt: a light check, like "Sorry?"
  • Second attempt: name the specific thing that was missed.
  • Third attempt: offer your best guess, or switch to spelling or keypad entry.
  • Treat silence as its own problem, with its own prompt.
  • Count failures across the whole call, and hand over to a person after about three.

How to build it

  • LogicKeep a repair counter for the whole call. When it reaches three, route to a human agent.
  • PromptGive the agent the repair sequence explicitly, and ban word-for-word repeats.
  • ChannelAdd expected names, product terms and codes to speech-recognition hints. Turn off filler-word removal where hesitation matters.
Principle 6

Switch channels when voice isn't the best tool

Some tasks are hard by voice: comparing more than three options, entering exact codes, giving the caller something to keep, collecting several precise fields, or recovering after repeated failed repairs.

When a task is one of these, move it to a better input. Text the caller a secure link, or ask them to use the keypad. Ask before switching, pre-fill anything you already know, and stay on the line while they finish. If you can look a value up from something you already know, like the caller's number, do that instead of asking them to spell it.

In practice

  • Switch for long lists, exact codes, several precise fields, or after repeated repair failures.
  • Ask before sending a text, and say which number it's going to.
  • Pre-fill the link with what the caller has already told you.
  • Stay on the call and confirm when the caller has finished.
  • Use the keypad for numbers, especially sensitive ones. Keypad digits arrive as exact data, so nothing gets misheard.

How to build it

  • LogicStore the caller's consent as a variable, and only send the text once it's set.
  • LogicRoute keypad input into the same capture logic as speech, since it arrives as plain text.
  • PromptTell the agent when to offer a channel switch and how to ask for consent.
Putting it into practice

Test with your ears, not your eyes

A script that reads well in a text preview can sound wrong on a call. Design the channel settings alongside the instructions, and test the way callers will actually experience the agent.

  • Listen to every flow as audio, not just as text.
  • Test on noisy lines, with different accents, and with people who haven't seen the script.
  • Turn each before/after pair into a regression scenario so fixes don't quietly break later.
  • Keep the split clear: turn rules in the prompt, sequence and counters in logic, sound and timing in channel settings.

Callers bring the same expectations to every call that they bring to any conversation. When an agent meets those expectations, the call simply feels natural.
